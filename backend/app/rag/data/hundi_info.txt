ABSTRACT
Despite the existence of legal and regulated systems, a significant proportion of Bangladeshi immigrants choose to utilize informal channels for the purpose of transferring funds. This practice has the potential to result in macroeconomic ramifications for Bangladesh. The rise of Hundi in Bangladesh has emerged as a noteworthy economic issue in recent times, particularly owing to its detrimental impact on the nation's foreign reserve and its utilization as a conduit for money launderers to unlawfully move funds abroad. The primary objective of this research is to analyze the implications of Hundi as a means of facilitating illicit financial operations. This study utilizes a qualitative research approach and a purposive sample consisting of 16 hundi dealers, 23 customers, and 7 senior bank officials working in the banking intelligence sector to investigate the hundi system's role in money laundering, despite the difficulties in accessing influential hundi dealers involved in significant transactions. The findings underscore the adverse economic consequences of Hundi, as it bypasses formal channels, thereby failing to contribute to Bangladesh's foreign reserves. Furthermore, it points out the exploitation of Hundi as a means for money laundering, enabling the assimilation of illicit funds into the legitimate economy, and supporting unlawful endeavors such as overseas property acquisition. The recommendations put forth suggest the implementation of strategies to enhance digital transactions, the establishment of a dedicated remittance application, and the design of a user-friendly agent banking system within the formal banking sector.
Introduction
Following the tragic acts of terrorism that occurred on September 11, 2001 in New York and Washington, D.C., the United States has heightened its state of alert with respect to informal funds transfer (IFT)1 mechanisms (El-Qorchi et al., 2003). The media consistently highlights the adverse association between IFT systems and the funding of terrorism, hence increasing governmental apprehensions regarding the possible exploitation of these systems for illicit financial activities (Maimbo, 2004). Criminal organizations have taken advantage of informal channels, characterized by their inherent anonymity and limited oversight, to facilitate the transfer of funds (Passas, 1999). Hundi is an example of an informal fund transfer system which is characterised by its emphasis on maintaining anonymity and circumvents the established framework of traditional banking systems (Hassan, 2022). Through alternative remittance networks, like the Hundi systems, illicit revenues from drug trafficking, funding of terrorist activities are frequently subjected to the process of money laundering (Ahmed, 2014). The inflow of remittances2 from abroad significantly contributes to the economic progress and poverty alleviation in Bangladesh, positioning the country as the seventh largest recipient globally (Daily Star, 2022). The primary origins of remittances received by Bangladesh are derived from its migrant3 labor force, predominantly situated in Middle Eastern countries, the United States, the United Kingdom, European nations, and East Asian countries (Raihan, 2023). The establishment of trade routes and increased migration has led to the emergence of Informal Funds Transfer (IFT) networks as a means to facilitate secure financial transactions across different geographical regions. This method was observed to have emerged throughout several regions including Asia, Africa, and the Middle East. Notably, each system was frequently identified by a unique geographical name4 (Thompson, 2019). The hundi system originated in the Indian subcontinent during the Mughal era, facilitated by local financiers who established extensive networks throughout India to facilitate revenue transmission back to Delhi due to geographical distance (Majumder, 2022). Bangladesh receives international remittances via both formal and informal sources. Formal channels refer to established banking systems and authorized methods of transferring money. The receipt of remittances via official channels plays a vital role in enabling the government to acquire foreign currencies, which is essential for upholding macroeconomic stability. However, concerns persist regarding a considerable proportion of these international remittances evading official channels and being transferred through informal means, primarily through taking advantage of the Hundi system (Raihan, 2023). Based on a survey conducted by the International Labour Organization (ILO), findings indicate that a substantial portion (40%) of remittances sent to Bangladesh by expatriates is facilitated through banking channels. Additionally, a noteworthy proportion (30%) of the overall remittance amount is directly contributed in the form of cash by expatriates or their family members. According to Majumder (2022), the hundi system is responsible for transmitting the remaining 30% of the total amount. The hundi system in Bangladesh is widely regarded as unlawful and works outside the established framework of conventional banking systems (Hassan, 2022). The utilisation of hundi as a means of transferring remittances has a notable consequence on the precision of official remittance data, hence impeding the government's capacity to accurately evaluate and efficiently manage its economic indicators. Furthermore, the anonymity and lack of transparency associated with hundi transactions give rise to apprehensions regarding potential engagement in unlawful endeavours, such as money laundering and the underground economy. According to economists, hundi is regarded as a significantly pernicious technique for facilitating money laundering in Bangladesh (Rahaman, 2021). The hundi system is a major source of money laundering and illicit remittances in the country, leading to significant revenue loss for the government. Additionally, the remittance of earnings by expatriates through Hundi does not directly contribute to the Bangladesh’s foreign exchange reserves of Central Bank, as noted by Majumder (2022). Hundi, operating outside conventional banking systems, has the potential to be exploited for illicit purposes like money laundering and terrorism financing. The unregulated use of Hundi by diaspora communities for remittances can exacerbate these issues, negatively impacting the economy and national security of Bangladesh. This study examines the negative repercussions of informal remittance methods, specifically the hundi system, on the economy of Bangladesh and its association with financial illicit activities. The introductory section of the paper draws attention to a decrease in formal remittances, despite a notable increase in the number of individuals working abroad. The literature review examines the historical backdrop and previous occurrences of the adverse use of hundi for the sake of terrorist financing and other illicit undertakings. The present study provides an analysis of the use of Hundi, encompassing its practiced method, illustrative case studies, and the obstacles faced by law enforcement agencies. The recommendation section presents a range of measures aimed at addressing the illicit utilisation of hundi, encompassing legislative upgrades and incentives for official banking channels.
Hundi being illegal with adverse impacts on economy
Despite a notable surge in the number of Bangladeshi individuals actively pursuing employment opportunities overseas, the anticipated corresponding rise in official remittance receipts has not increased. Based on statistics provided by the Bureau of Manpower Employment and Training and Bangladesh Bank, it was observed that in 2015, a cumulative count of 555,881 individuals from Bangladesh undertook migration for the specific objective of seeking employment opportunities. As a result of this migration, the country received remittances amounting to $15.27 billion. Nevertheless, in the year 2016, despite the substantial migration of 757,731 workers, there was a notable decline of 10.9% in the inflow of remittances, which amounted to $13.61 billion. In a parallel manner, it can be observed that in the year 2021, despite a notable surge in the number of Bangladeshis, reaching a historic high of 11,35,873 individuals, who actively pursued employment opportunities abroad, the overall inflow of remittances experienced a decline of 1.5% when compared to the previous year, resulting in a total remittance value of $21.25 billion. According to Palma (2023), the government commonly ascribes this disparity to migrants' inclination towards utilizing informal channels for money transfer, which are characterized by their simplicity and consequently yield reduced official remittance statistics. Experts attribute Bangladesh's decline in foreign exchange reserves to the hundi cartel, arguing that the disparity in exchange rates between formal and informal channels motivates remitters to engage in illicit cross-border financial transactions (Uddin, 2022). From 2004–2021, the amount of money held by Bangladeshis in Swiss banks has been increasing, indicating a rise in money smuggling from Bangladesh to foreign countries. In 2019, the amount was BDT 5367 crore (approximately 636 million USD), slightly decreasing to BDT 5347 crore (approximately 633 million USD) in 2020. However, in 2021, the amount increased significantly to BDT 8275 crore (approximately 980 million USD) (Chowdhury, 2022). Based on a report published by Prothom Alo in 2022, Bangladesh encounters an annual financial setback of over $7.8 billion (Tk 750 billion) in remittances as a result of illicit money laundering endeavors conducted by an estimated 5,000 agents affiliated with Mobile Financial Services (MFS) functioning within the nation. Based on a report published by Transparency International Bangladesh, it has been estimated that around $3.1 billion or Tk 26.4 thousand crore is illicitly transported out of Bangladesh on a yearly basis. According to a report from New Age (2023), during the period of July to September 2022, it was observed that hundi agents illicitly diverted an estimated amount of Tk 25,000 crore in remittances. Literature review
Informal remittance systems have historically been deeply embedded within the socio-economic framework of South Asia, encompassing a significant period of time. During the 1950 s and 1960 s, the primary modes of performing financial transactions in the Indian subcontinent encompassed hundi, chiti, and hawala. The aforementioned procedures involved the utilization of a draft that was issued based on a trustworthy trading partner (El-Qorchi et al., 2003). Migrants have utilized the hundi system for a range of factors, encompassing trustworthiness, cost-effectiveness, promptness, convenience, and accessibility. Furthermore, the hundi system provides certain individuals with the benefit of maintaining anonymity for both the senders and recipients of funds (Maimbo et al., 2005). El-Qorchi et al. (2003) asserted recent studies suggest that the growth of the hundi network can be attributed in part to its involvement in gold trade and smuggling activities that were prevalent in South Asia throughout the 1960 s and 1970 s. In Sharma's (2006) study, a comprehensive operational framework for Hundi transactions was presented, encompassing all countries where Hundi or Hawala dealers are active. The study emphasized the importance of maintaining significant cash reserves to ensure efficient execution of transactions in the local currency within a 24-hour timeframe. The researcher highlights the presence of potentially illicit funds, including blood money, proceeds from corrupt activities, and untraceable or illicitly obtained funds, within these local financial reserves. Therefore, in the event that an individual engages in illegitimate financial transactions, it is possible that the funds being used for such payments have been derived from illicit activities such as extortion, kidnapping, or other forms of organized criminal operations. Corruption and the shadow economy in India are brought to light by Raj (1998), who estimates that they could account for as much as 40% of India's GDP. The Hundi system is utilized as a mechanism to ease the transfer of illicit cash across international borders. In addition, it is important to note that Pakistani criminal networks have formed alliances with Afghan drug traffickers in order to engage in the transit of illegal substances and the illicit trade of items, which facilitates the distribution of these goods from Afghanistan to various worldwide markets. Alternative remittance systems, such as Hundi, have been linked to money laundering of narcotics trafficking profits and the financing of terrorist activities (Haider, 2014). There were suspicions surrounding the potential use of Hundi as a means for terrorists to redirect a portion of the funds used in the September 11, 2001 attacks. However, there was a lack of complete verification regarding these reports. Subsequent to the terrorist strikes targeting the United States on September 11, 2001, there has been a notable increase in scholarly research conducted on Hundi. As stated in the 9/11 Commission Report of 2004, “Al Qaeda frequently moved the money it raised by hawala/ hundi, an informal and ancient trust-based system for transferring funds” (9/11 Commission Report, 2004, p. 171). In the Indian context, it has been observed that Hundi has been employed as a financial instrument to support acts of terrorism in regions such as Jammu and Kashmir, as well as the northeastern states of India, for an extended period of time. In the year 1997, during an interrogation, Abdul Majid Dar disclosed that a prominent figure within the Hurriyat organization had encouraged him to establish a textile business and receive funds on behalf of their group. The monetary value associated with this Hundi transaction was approximately $22,000. In a separate event that took place in 1997, the supreme commander of Hizbul Mujahideen was involved in the facilitation of financial transfers to Kashmir via Dubai (Jamwal, 2002). Hundi is used in Bangladesh to send money abroad illegally through mobile financial services and apps (Nishan, 2022). This includes money obtained through extortion, bribery, and corruption, as well as foreign currency obtained by inflating export prices and understating import prices. A suspected movement of non-banking fund from a neighboring country to Bangladesh is being investigated into by the Counter Terrorism and Transnational Crime (CTTC) unit of the Dhaka Metropolitan Police (DMP), Bangladesh. The unit is currently searching for militant leaders who received 10 million taka from India in 2023 (approximately 118,000 USD). The funds have been transmitted to both the new and old terrorist groups Ansar al-Islam (ABT) and Jama'atul Mujahideen Bangladesh (JMB). The funds were initially transferred from Malaysia to a Middle Eastern nation, converted into gold, and then transferred to India before reaching extremist organization-affiliated individuals via hundi. Due to a lack of information about the senders or recipients, the unit is unable to trace the individuals involved in these suspicious transactions. The conventional framework for money laundering, encompassing three sequential phases known as placement, layering, and integration, might serve as a theoretical framework for comprehending the use of the hundi system in the illicit money laundering process. The initial stage in integrating illicit funds into the lawful financial framework involves strategic placement. Within the framework of the hundi system, such a scenario may occur when an individual residing abroad entrusts funds to a hundi agent with the intention of facilitating a transfer, or alternatively, when a dishonest individual provides unlawfully acquired cash to a hundi agent. During the process of layering, hundi agents employ a series of complex financial transactions to obscure the source of funds and enhance the complexity of their traceability. The act of depositing funds within the hundi system involves the utilization of several hundi agents and transactions, hence rendering the identification of the source of transferred cash challenging. The third step, known as integration, entails re-introducing the money that has been laundered into the formal economy in a way that makes it appear as though the money was obtained legally. This might happen in the hundi system when the money is sent to the person to whom it is intended (such as the expatriate's family in Bangladesh), and then it gets spent in the formal economy. It could also occur when a hundi agent uses expatriate funds to purchase property in a foreign country on behalf of a corrupt individual.
Methodology
In order to acquire an in-depth grasp of the hundi system and its part in the process of money laundering, a qualitative research approach was applied for the purpose of this study. Complex social phenomena, as well as the intricacies of participant experiences, are most effectively explored through qualitative research (Stutterheim and Ratcliffe, 2021). The major approach employed for data gathering involved conducting semi-structured interviews with a purposive sample of participants who had direct experience with the hundi system. These participants included hundi dealers, customers, and senior bank officials from Bangladesh bank financial intelligence unit and the security section unit officers from both private and public banks. The participants were chosen for the study based on their level of experience as well as their participation in hundi transactions. This was done to ensure that the participants would be able to offer insightful commentary regarding the functioning and behavior of the system. The researchers used the principle of data saturation to determine the size of the sample, including 16 hundi dealers, 23 customers, and 7 senior bank officials. Additionally, we aimed to include 2 officers working in Bangladesh bank financial intelligence unit, 4 officers working in the security unit of private banks, and 1 officer from a public bank of Bangladesh. Data saturation is a concept that pertains to the stage in data collection wherein no additional information or themes arise, signifying that the sample size adequately captures the range of perspectives and experiences within the target population (Francis et al., 2010). The data collected from interviews was transcribed verbatim, ensuring that the exact words spoken by participants were accurately recorded. Subsequently, a thematic analysis was conducted on the transcriptions. During the study process, ethical considerations played a significant role. Confidentiality and anonymity were consistently upheld throughout the study to safeguard the identities of the participants and protect sensitive information. The purpose of the study was to provide a nuanced and in-depth understanding of the function that the hundi system plays in money laundering as well as the operational complexities of the system by applying this comprehensive qualitative research approach.
Findings
The decrease in foreign exchange reserves of the Bangladesh Bank and the subsequent inflationary pressures on essential commodities can be attributed to the prevailing dollar crisis. Based on data sourced from the Bangladesh Bank and the Bureau of Manpower, Employment and Training (BMET), it is estimated that approximately 10 million citizens from Bangladesh currently reside in foreign nations. During the period spanning from July 2022 to March 2023, expatriates as remittances transferred a sum of $16.03 billion (equivalent to 5.21 billion taka). During the preceding period spanning from 2021 to 2022, the total value of remittances reached $2.13 billion. In contrast, the pandemic year of 2020–2021 witnessed an unprecedented influx of remittances amounting to $204.77 billion, resulting in a notable surge in Bangladesh's reserve to $48.06 billion. This study highlights the role of Hundi as a notable contributing factor to the decrease in remittances, as well as its exploitation by money launderers for the purpose of engaging in illicit money transfers from the context of Bangladesh.
Method of transferring money through Hundi
The use of Hundi to transfer illegally obtained funds, evade tax obligations, or conceal monetary transactions from authorities can be classified as financial misconduct. Aside from that, many Bangladeshis illegally reside abroad. Hundi is their only option for money transfers. Because without a valid visa, formal banking is impossible. Consequently, Hundi becomes their last resort (Hassan, 2022). These activities have the potential to adversely affect the formal banking sector, compromise the financial stability of the country, and hinder the government's capacity to enforce regulatory measures and facilitate tax collection. The Hundi money transfer process generally entails the sender entrusting funds to a Hundi dealer situated in one location, who subsequently establishes communication with another Hundi dealer located in the recipient's location in order to facilitate the delivery of an equivalent sum to the intended recipient. The hundi market of Bangladesh can be analyzed from two distinct perspectives. Firstly, it is evident that there is a clear demand for the Bangladeshi currency, referred to as the taka, among overseas workers who intend to send money back to their home country. Moreover, there is a substantial demand for the United States dollar, stemming from several variables such as tourism, medical expenses, international education, registration fees, and criminal activities including money laundering and property acquisition. Individuals or groups seeking to transfer their unlawfully obtained funds overseas engage in a process whereby they remit the illicit funds in the local currency, taka, to local hundi dealers or their representatives. In return, the hundi dealer located abroad deposits an equivalent sum of money in US dollars into specified accounts belonging to said individuals or groups. The funds acquired by local hundi operators from individuals involved in money laundering are utilized to pay the families of remitters in Bangladesh, in return for the US dollars received from said remitters residing in foreign nations. The hundi network facilitates the transfer of funds by individuals who engage in illicit capital outflows from the country. Expatriates prefer to send money through hundi due to its better exchange rate compared to banks. Despite the government's 2.5% incentive, expatriates still find higher exchange rates in the hundi, leading them to prefer it over banks (Islam, 2022). A case study involving a hundi transaction can serve as a valuable illustration for elucidating the procedural aspects of such transactions. The study included a hundi dealer who shared examples of a case he managed. The case study focused on Ali, an individual employed at a restaurant in New York City, who desired to remit his earnings to his wife, Aisha, residing in Sylhet, Bangladesh. Ali has conducted an observation indicating that exchange houses in his locality, which deal to Bangladeshi currency, provide a conversion rate of 100 Bangladeshi Taka for one United States dollar. The exchange houses charge a transaction fee amounting to $2. Moreover, Ali is obligated to invest both temporal and monetary resources towards commuting to Manhattan. Unfortunately, due to the considerable distance between his place of employment and Manhattan, as well as the lack of approval from his supervisor to engage in personal activities during designated working hours, the individual is unable to carry out personal responsibilities. Additionally, Ali is required to prepare an official document and wait one week for confirmation that the money has been successfully transferred to the bank account chosen for his wife, Aisha. If Ali were to transfer $100, the net value obtained by Aisha's family would be 10,000 Taka less than the original amount after accounting for Ali's expenses, which encompass the fee, travel costs, and postal charges. Nevertheless, Ali possesses knowledge regarding one of his colleagues, Hassan, who is a reputable hundi dealer. Hassan expresses his willingness to exchange 10,600 Taka for $100, without any supplementary conditions. Ali makes the decision to transfer a sum of $100 to Hassan, who promptly initiates communication with his brother Imran residing in Sylhet. Hassan provides Imran with explicit instructions to promptly deliver the specified monetary amount to Aisha within a time frame of thirty minutes. Aisha expresses great delight upon receiving the remittance without any complications, resulting in an additional sum of 600 Taka. Given Ali's rationality as an economic agent,5 it is probable that he would opt for the hundi route presented by Hassan. The alternative remittance technique offers a higher overall value and effectively addresses the difficulties, fees, and time lags typically associated with conventional banking channels. In several regions across the globe, the hundi industry is subject to relatively limited regulation. Consequently, hundi dealers operating in locations such as the USA, Europe, or elsewhere are able to conduct their business with minimal oversight. This advantageous situation enables hundi dealers like Hasan, who operate in foreign jurisdictions, to evade legal constraints. In another case, a comprehensive inquiry carried out by the Criminal Investigation Department (CID) revealed that hundi dealers, who were active in Dubai and Saudi Arabia, accumulated a significant 5 An economic agent refers to an individual, organization, or entity that makes decisions and takes actions based on economic considerations. The findings of the investigation indicate that the hundi gangs are organized into three discrete factions. The initial cohort is tasked with the collection of foreign currency from individuals who are living outside their home country. The funds are collected from individuals who wish to facilitate the transfer of money to their designated recipients in their country of origin. The second cohort of hundi traders engages in the process of remitting the accumulated funds to Mobile Financial Service (MFS) agents using the domestic currency. These agents serve as intermediaries and facilitate the process of converting foreign currency into the desired local currency. The third category consists of MFS agents who are responsible for receiving funds from hundi traders. The hundi system, which has traditionally relied on physical means and trusted intermediaries, is now influenced by technology and digital platforms. Some operators have established online platforms for electronic transactions, offering convenience and speed. However, these platforms are not popular in Bangladesh. Some operators are exploring blockchain technology and cryptocurrencies for cross-border transactions, offering enhanced security and transparency. The emergence of digital hundi transactions has raised regulatory challenges as they blur the lines between informal and formal financial systems. The Chief of CID (Criminal Investigation Department) reported that hundi operators are constantly updating their systems and using mobile apps for transactions. Saudi expatriate hundi operators are using mobile apps like "Freedom-Flexi24.com" to send money to Bangladesh, with the help of MFSA agents. The funds are distributed directly to recipients through Bangladeshi hundi agents (Nishan, 2022). The arrested individuals were involved in illegal hundi activities, smuggling money from abroad without bringing it into Bangladesh, paying taxes in local currency to avoid customs duties, using VPNs (Virtual Private Networks) to carry out these activities.
A case study of Dilkusha Hundi business of Dhaka
The hundi dealers in Dilkusha are involved in facilitating money laundering operations, which persisted for a certain duration, flourishing within the vibrant commercial center of Dhaka. Within the vibrant Dilkusha area of Dhaka, Bangladesh, a covert network of hundi traders conduct their commercial activities disguised as currency exchange agents. Hundi dealers operate small, unobtrusive establishments disguised as lawful currency exchange businesses. They use sophisticated mechanisms to facilitate fund transfers, leveraging their extensive networks across countries. Situated in Dilkusha's commercial center, their business exploits the market demand for informal money transfer services, despite the appearance of a legitimate enterprise. In Dilkusha, a clandestine network of hundi dealers has emerged, catering to individuals involved in money laundering activities. These dealers, skilled in intricate networks, facilitate the smooth and confidential transfer of financial resources. They receive large amounts of illicit funds from money launderers and transform them into lawful currencies, concealing their illicit origin. Their primary responsibility is to integrate these funds into the legitimate financial system, preventing authorities from trace their origin. Despite the presence of the Bangladesh Bank, the central bank of Bangladesh, and other established financial institutions in the same area, the informal Hundi enterprise has flourished in Dilkusha, Dhaka.
Involved agents in the Hundi transaction process
Anyone who helps a criminal launder money is considered a financial facilitator (e.g., a Hundi dealer) (Soudijn, 2012). The Criminal Investigation Department (CID) in Dhaka has identified over a thousand illegal money exchangers operating in the country, engaging in fraudulent activities related to foreign exchange market manipulation. These individuals move hundreds of crores of taka in transactions daily, much of which is laundered in foreign countries. Police arrested 14 individuals and seized Tk 2 crore from multiple illegal money exchangers, including Tk 1.11 crore in currencies from 19 different nations. The research reveals that some money launderers exploit the uncertainty surrounding the foreign exchange market and contribute to a currency shortage caused by the Covid-19 outbreak and the conflict between Russia and Ukraine. The Financial Intelligence Unit and the central bank of Bangladesh are also concerned about legitimate money changers participating in the hundi black market. Hundi as a tool to conducted illegal activities
The Hundi system, which operates outside the legal framework of money transfers, is commonly utilized for the unauthorized diversion of government funds and their subsequent transfer abroad. Illicit capital flight is prevalent among business figures, government officials, and private sector executives who transfer funds overseas, posing risks. A quotation from a person working as a money exchanger and occasionally a hundi dealer highlights recent fraudulent schemes and illicit financial activities involving money laundering: Recently, prominent cases of scams and money laundering, including those involving Hallmark,6 Destiny,7 and P.K. Halder's alleged embezzlement8 of BDT 11,000 crore, have gained significant attention. These cases highlight the utilization of hundi as a means to obscure and misplace illicit funds. The reason behind resorting to hundi in such money laundering instances is the perceived risk of detection when conducting transactions through traditional banking channels. The decline in the nation's foreign reserve can primarily be attributed to the utilization of hundi by businesspersons to augment their export-related profits. Furthermore, it has been noted by experts that traders employ supplementary tax avoidance tactics, including the practice of under-invoicing imports in order to reduce the amount of taxes paid. In order to finance their imports, a significant number of importers employ a combination of hundi transfers and letters of credit.9 A significant portion of hundi transactions, or the illicit acquisition of dollars, is employed for the purpose of transferring funds across international borders that have been acquired through illegal means by political affiliates. Additionally, remitters utilize the hundi services to transfer money to their home country. Hundi service providers engage in the collection of foreign currency from remitters in order to fulfill the corresponding demand. Challenges for the law enforcement agency members
The Hundi system operates through a network of intermediaries or agents that facilitate the transfer of funds without the need for physical movement of money. These agents are usually community members who are trusted and have a deep understanding of local economies and cultural norms. Transactions in the Hundi system are based on trust, honor, and longstanding relationships, which hinders the ability to trace and monitor these transactions due to the scarcity of documentation. The following quote provided by a senior private bank official emphasizes the challenges associated with identifying illicit hundi transactions: One of the worst ways to hide money is through Hundi. If someone tries to launder money through import or export, they have to show different kinds of papers. So, the name of the person who did it is found right away. But in Hundi, most money is moved through middlemen. Everything is based on trust, and no paperwork is needed to move money. If money is brought in this way, it is hard to figure out who is doing it. Hundi facilitates a considerable degree of anonymity for both the sender and recipient of funds, thereby posing challenges for law enforcement agencies in their efforts to ascertain the identities of the parties engaged in such transactions. Hundi transactions frequently encompass multiple nations, thereby introducing complexities to the processes of investigation and prosecution. Hundi practices are subject to varying legal frameworks and regulations across different nations, necessitating international collaboration for the efficient mitigation of such activities.
Discussion
Hundi might be likened to the clandestine realm of the internet commonly referred to as the "dark web." The dark web is frequently likened to the submerged portion of an iceberg in the ocean, where only a little portion is visible above the surface while its extensive magnitude remains concealed beneath. Hundi's circumstance has certain similarities. Financial institutions employ a distinct methodology to determine the annual remittances made by migrants. Nevertheless, an accurate estimation of the total amount of money being illicitly transported by hundi on an annual basis, as well as the precise quantity of funds entering the market, remains elusive (Maruf, 2022). The increase of Bangladesh's foreign reserves adds to the country's economic growth. This is because the reserves may be used to pay for prospective future imports, which in turn improves the overall economic well-being of the nation. Nevertheless, in the scenario of a migrant individual who utilizes Hundi traders to transfer funds to Bangladesh, it is observed that their transferred dollars do not ultimately reach the Bangladesh Bank. Consequently, it is not making a positive contribution to the national economy. The utilization of hundi facilitates the expansion of an informal economy, wherein transactions take place beyond the scope of official regulations and taxation. The presence of the informal sector poses challenges to the government's tax collection efforts, distorts economic indicators, and diminishes the overall transparency and accountability of economic activities. The utilization of hundi by Bangladeshi remitters can be ascribed to various factors, encompassing the aspiration to optimize the worth of their diligently earned income. Similar to other economic actors, remitters aim to minimize expenses and optimize the quantity of funds transferred to their families residing in Bangladesh. Hundi provides more favorable exchange rates in comparison to official channels, thereby guaranteeing that remitters receive enhanced value for their monetary transactions. On the other hand, it is commonly observed that banks tend to offer exchange rates that are comparatively lower in quantity and impose additional charges for the provision of their services. The perceived convenience and simplicity that hundi offers, especially for immigrants with lower levels of education, contributes to its popularity. The conventional banking system can present linguistic obstacles and involve intricate paperwork procedures, prompting numerous expatriates to choose hundi as a more expedient and convenient alternative for remitting funds to their country of origin. Regrettably, the inherent convenience of the hundi system also presents an avenue for money launderers to exploit, thereby enabling the utilization of remitters' funds for illicit activities. In order to give the appearance of legitimacy, illicit proceeds from a variety of criminal enterprises, including but not limited to drug trafficking, human trafficking, are frequently subjected to money laundering techniques within the financial system. Although hundi serves legitimate purposes, it is also susceptible to exploitation by individuals engaged in illicit financial activities. Also in the context of Bangladesh, money launderers exploit the informal nature and absence of documentation within the hundi system in order to obscure the sources of illicit funds. The utilization of a network of hundi operators enables the execution of numerous transactions, resulting in intricate layers of intricacy that pose difficulties in tracing the initial origin of the funds. The hundi system has the potential to serve as a channel for the assimilation of illegitimate funds into the legitimate economy, encompassing both overseas residents and individuals engaged in corrupt practices. At first, expatriates employed in foreign nations exhibit a preference for utilizing the hundi system as a means of remitting funds to Bangladesh. The individuals initiate communication with a hundi agent and convey their intention to initiate a financial transfer. The hundi agent provides a guarantee to the expatriate that the funds will be transferred to their family members in Bangladesh without the requirement of any formal paperwork. Second, the hundi dealer acts as an intermediary by getting in touch with another hundi agent in Bangladesh. The hundi trader gives the agent explicit instructions to wire a certain sum of money to the expatriate’s family members back home. But the hundi agent does not use the expatriate’s money, but rather money taken from fraudulent locals who need to make international wire transfers or convert their black money into legal currency. This Placement of money refers to the steps used to introduce substantial sums of dirty money into the banking system, such as through structured deposits or the mixing in of these funds with legal business revenues (Cassella, 2018). Thirdly, in Bangladesh, the hundi agent facilitates the transfer of funds to the family members of expatriates, utilizing illicit funds provided by corrupt individuals. The primary objective of expatriates is to remit funds to their family members, while corrupt individuals in Bangladesh engage with local Hundi dealers to facilitate the illicit transfer of their funds to foreign jurisdictions, thereby engaging in money laundering activities. The process of money laundering described herein necessitates substantial expertise and resources. Perpetrators of such illicit activities often enlist the aid of individuals known as financial facilitators, such as hundi dealers. Experts with specialized skills and expertise are integral members of criminal networks, as highlighted by Kleemans et al. (2002). Consequently, the illicitly acquired funds, commonly referred to as black money, are successfully integrated into the legitimate economic system of Bangladesh through their utilization by the family members of expatriates. The hundi system is utilized in this process as a way to transfer funds without proper documentation. It involves using the funds of expatriates and corrupt individuals to integrate black money into the formal economy and enable illicit activities. In the same process of transferring illegal money through hundi, any person having the intention of terrorist financing can send money easily as the hundi system does not require any formal documentation and it lacks proper security measures. Hundi is primarily accountable for the adverse consequences associated with expatriate income. In order to enhance the existing conditions of the nation, it is crucial to increase vigilance in the areas that enable the practice of hundi. The utilization of hundi is particularly widespread among prominent entrepreneurs, government officials, and individuals employed in the private sector who encounter potential hazards in retaining their illicitly obtained funds within the nation and instead opt to clandestinely transfer them overseas.
Recommendation
The individuals utilizing hundi services are often unaware of the fact that engaging in hundi transactions is considered illegal. Migrant workers who engage in employment overseas encounter minimal ethical dilemmas and typically have limited leisure time available. A comprehensive strategy is necessary to address the issue of money laundering through hundi. This encompasses enhancing the regulatory and supervisory framework of the formal financial sector, enhancing the availability of banking services, and enhancing public knowledge regarding the hazards and ramifications associated with the utilization of informal money transfer mechanisms. Furthermore, international collaboration is necessary due to the frequent involvement of crossborder transfers in hundi transactions. To enhance their appeal to remitters, it is recommended that banks make efforts to offer exchange rates that are more competitive. Banks has the capacity to incentivize customers to utilize formal channels for their remittance needs by providing more advantageous exchange rates in contrast to hundi services. The government may consider the potential implementation of further incentives, in conjunction with the previously announced 2.5% incentive for formal remittances. The government's efforts to encourage digital transactions and develop a cashless economy have the potential to mitigate criminal activities associated with Hundi. The promotion of electronic payment systems, including mobile banking, online transfers, and digital wallets, offers individuals and organizations the opportunity to decrease their reliance on physical transaction. The growing prevalence of cashless transactions has led to a decrease in the likelihood of illicit activities, such as Hundi, that rely on untraceable cash transfers. Moreover, the creation of a remittance related application that enables seamless transfers between different financial systems possesses the capacity to significantly augment the effectiveness and clarity of the remittance procedure. Consequently, this can contribute to a decrease in reliance on hundi operations. The development of a safe and user-friendly gateway facilitates the convenient transmission of remittances, eliminating the need for intermediaries and bypassing prolonged processes. By implementing such a system, the remittance process can be streamlined as it eliminates the requirement for funds to go through multiple stages, including the head office and branches. A remittance-based app can effectively meet the needs of a diverse population by capitalizing on the success and widespread usage of platforms such as bkash. Conclusion
If the economic cancer hundi cannot be stopped, it will keep impeding the nation's economic growth and advancement. The fight against the use of hundi in Bangladesh is being waged with the intention of preserving national security, the legitimacy of the country's monetary system, and international anti-money laundering standards. People inside the nation utilize the hundi business to shadily transfer their income abroad, in addition to expatriates who use it to send money home through shadowy means. There will always be a need for hundi as long as these transfer routes from Bangladesh are not blocked.

Gone with the hundi Hundi continues to rob the nation of billions. Here’s how agent banking can help

https://www.dhakatribune.com/305070 Gone with the hundi Ashraful Alam ChowdhuryAshraful Alam Chowdhury Publish : 17 Feb 2023, 04:06 AMUpdate : 17 Feb 2023, 01:08 PM Every time analysts discuss the factors that contribute to money being transferred to other nations illegally, they highlight two significant points: Trade based money transfer -- under invoicing and over invoicing -- as the first, and use of hundi as the second. 

The problem of trade-based money laundering is being addressed by Bangladesh Bank (BB) through a specific audit. But the real challenge open today is: How to limit the use of hundi? If the usage of hundi could be restricted, together with increased efforts by Bangladesh Bank to curtail trade-based money laundering, the country's current economic struggles could be resolved earlier than anticipated.


Remittances are one of the biggest sources of foreign exchange, as is common knowledge. This aids in reducing Bangladesh's current account deficit in the country's payment balance. Remittance inflow, however, is significantly out of proportion to the number of Bangladeshi employees who live abroad. 

For instance, in 2022, Bangladesh sent the most migrant labourers ever, but remittance receipts did not increase accordingly. Though, last year, more than 11.35 lakh Bangladeshis flew out of the nation in search of employment overseas, nearly tripling the 6.17 lakh migrant workers who did so the year before, surprisingly, the amount of money that employees moved through the official channel fell 6.65% from $22 billion to $21.28bn in 2022. 

This happened mainly for two reasons: First, after being hired in a host nation, new Bangladeshi migrant workers often go through a three-month probationary term. As a result, it takes them three to six months to start sending money home. The usage of the unauthorized portal, ie, hundi, is the second cause of the disparity.

In the hundi system, local agents distribute or collect money on behalf of friends, family members, or other agents without the benefit of legal protection or oversight, with the understanding that any outstanding debts will be cleared through subsequent transactions. 

As stated in the definition, hundi has no legal foundation or binding. In contrast, sending money through a bank is safer since it offers legal support. 

Even after that, the use of hundi is growing alarmingly. Reports indicate that only 51% of remittances to Bangladesh are sent in a formal, legal manner; the remaining 49% are sent through hundi. The reason why hundi is attracting more users lies in the benefits that hundi offers.

Compared to traditional banking, hundi is quicker and more practical. Documents are needed for official banking, and there are formalities. The complexities in banking procedure is challenging, especially for the rural people, while the door-to-door service is one of hundi's practical advantages. 

Locals can obtain money without traveling to distant branches. Additionally, the hundi exchange rate is greater than the banking foreign exchange rate. Furthermore, there are illegal immigrants who do not comply with the legal requirements to send money home. 

The sum of all these factors leads migrants to prefer hundi over banks.

Bangladesh Bank continues to make efforts to encourage migrants to adopt the official route. To promote remittance inflow, it has loosened its regulations. Even the incentives went up to 2.5%. This undoubtedly was helpful to some extent. The most recent period, January 2023, saw a about $1.96bn inflow of remittances. However, a sizable amount of money is still moving through non-banking channels. 

Agent banking could be useful in bringing those monies into the financial system.

How agent banking could help

Agent banking refers to the provision of small scale financial and banking services to the underserved community by engaged agents under a legal agency arrangement. The activities and use of agent banking is increasing day by day as the rural people are becoming more familiar with it. 

According to the Bangladesh Bank, there are 20,177 outlets managing more than 16 million accounts (14.47 million rural accounts) depositing Tk306bn. The inward remittance collection has also increased to Tk95.81bn which is 9.72% higher than the previous quarter. 

Agent banking has collected around 16% of the total remittance inflow of Tk595.58bn through the banking channel. More significantly, the major share of the remittances collected through agent banking is going to the rural areas, which is expected to rejuvenate the rural economy.

Though agent banking is collecting inward remittance, it still has a lot to explore. 

The underprivileged areas of Bangladesh have been experiencing agent banking but it is still less convenient for the workers who live abroad.  It is necessary to establish more agent banking locations outside of the nation, particularly in the Middle East where there are more migrants. 

Then, to match the hundi exchange rate, the incentives for remittance could be increased. And if it's possible, agent banking might be linked with Mobile Financial Services (Bkash, Nagad, etc) rather than adding more outlets around the nation to cut costs and maximize the use of the available resources.

Once agent banking offers similar conveniences, it will have the advantage over hundi because of its legality.

If these suggestions are put into practice, agent banking can successfully replace the use of hundi and increase remittances. It will lessen the strain on currency reserves and gradually lower inflation.
